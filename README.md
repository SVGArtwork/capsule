# Capsule

Capsule is an interactive illustrated capsule wardrobe experience built as a Progressive Web App.

The project is centered around a complex inline SVG illustration representing a wardrobe and a model.  
Users can combine clothing items by interacting directly with the illustration and receive playful visual feedback.

Capsule is not a traditional game: there is no score, no winning or losing.  
The interaction itself — exploring combinations and visual outcomes — is the experience.

[![Capsule preview](assets/capsule-preview.png)](https://svgartwork.github.io/capsule/)


## Features

- Inline SVG illustration with high visual detail
- JavaScript-driven interactions
- Progressive Web App (PWA)
- Offline-capable logic via Service Worker
- Designed primarily for landscape orientation

## Technology

- HTML
- CSS
- JavaScript
- Inline SVG
- Progressive Web App APIs (Manifest, Service Worker)

No backend, no database.

## Live Demo

https://svgartwork.github.io/capsule/

## Notes

Capsule is conceived as a visual-first, exploratory experience.  
It works best on larger screens or in landscape orientation.

## Drawing, Time, and Interaction
## An interactive SVG illustration project between experience and education

## The Origin of a Project
This project originates from a simple and persistent desire: to draw.
A desire that never disappeared, but that for long periods could only express itself intermittently, at the margins of a professional path that moved in a very different direction.
This discontinuity is not a biographical detail: it has deeply shaped the way I work and the way I think about creative practice.
For many years, drawing remained an activity cultivated in reduced spaces, carved out within contexts that were not designed to host it. In those conditions, drawing became less a productive tool and more a way of maintaining contact with what felt most essential to me. Not an escape, but a quiet form of resistance.
In this sense, tools such as Inkscape, the web, and vector drawing were not simply technical means. They were spaces of possibility. Accessible, tolerated environments that allowed me to keep creating even under strong constraints. They did not remove limitations, but they made it possible to move through them without completely interrupting my relationship with creativity. Their value lay not in power or efficiency, but in the continuity they made possible.
The project takes shape precisely within these constraints. It does not emerge from an abundance of resources, but from the decision to invest time, attention, and effort into detail, using what was available.
The slowness of drawing, the insistence on care, the patient work on complex images are not decorative elements: they directly determine the type of experience offered to the user.
The underlying idea is simple and radical at the same time: to make the medium disappear, so that attention is not captured by the tool, but by the experience itself.
This approach also influences how the project relates to education. Learning here is not conceived as the rapid transmission of content, but as exploration.
Unlike a video, which imposes its own timing, or a text, which requires an immediate effort of decoding, an interactive drawing can afford to wait. It can pause, leave space, invite action without forcing it. It asks the user to do something, but does not compel; it accompanies without rigid guidance.
At the same time, precisely because it is interactive, it does not allow itself to be merely observed. In this space of interaction, information and content emerge while the user acts.
There is no guarantee that everything will be absorbed, and this risk is an integral part of the project.
The goal is not to measure, evaluate, or classify, but to render the user active, involved, present.
For this reason, the visual aspect cannot be merely functional. Asking for time and attention implies responsibility: if someone is invited into a place they might not enter spontaneously, that place has a duty to be welcoming, carefully constructed, generous — not to seduce, but to be honest.
This project is educational because it does not tell you what to do, nor does it judge how you do it. It invites you to play, to explore, and offers an experience as a gift. It is up to the person who enters to decide whether to accept it.

## The Experience: What the User Sees and Does
Entering the experience does not happen through a menu or an instruction, but through a scene. The user is immediately faced with a complex illustration: a large Art Deco–style wardrobe with glass doors. Behind the glass, clothes can be glimpsed hanging, suggesting the presence of content that is not yet fully accessible.
At the center of the scene appears a smiling cartoon-style girl who looks directly at the user. She wears jeans and a white T-shirt with the words Herald Tribune. Her short blonde hair and overall attitude evoke the imagery of actress Jean Seberg. Behind her are other elements: a Liberty-style decorative screen, an armchair with a lamp beside it, a black cat sleeping, and a Persian rug. The environment is rich, designed to be observed before being used.
When the project opens, no explicit instructions are provided. The user does not immediately understand what they are supposed to do, but can intuit that some elements are interactive: as the mouse moves across the scene, clickable areas reveal themselves, suggesting possible actions without imposing them.
The space has a narrative nature. It is neither abstract nor purely functional, but constructed through cultural and stylistic references that contribute to its atmosphere. It is an environment that invites observation first, and interaction only afterward, allowing the user time to visually orient themselves.
Available actions produce immediate and visible effects. Clicking on the girl changes her position: she disappears from the front and reappears from behind, while keeping her face turned toward the user. This device allows clothing to be observed both from the front and the back, making observation an integral part of interaction.
The wardrobe doors, organized into modules, open and close with a sliding movement, making the relationship between gesture and result explicit. Clothes selected from the wardrobe appear directly on the model, allowing the user to compose different combinations and progressively modify the character’s appearance.
There is no declared objective and no prescribed direction. The experience unfolds through attempts, variations, and repetition.
Some combinations trigger reactions within the scene. The cat, initially asleep, becomes a silent but expressive observer: when an outfit is perceived as successful, it opens its eyes and a speech bubble with a heart appears; at the same time, the lamp beside the armchair lights up. When the combination is less convincing, the cat expresses disapproval with a speech bubble showing a fish skeleton. These signals do not block the experience and do not introduce scoring; they provide narrative and ironic feedback.
Textual content of an informational or educational nature appears through modal windows. Its consultation is always optional: the user may read it, ignore it, or return to it later without compromising the experience.
There is no time pressure. The user can pause, observe, go back, repeat combinations indefinitely. It is possible to zoom in to explore details of the illustration, further emphasizing the centrality of looking and dwelling. The experience does not push forward; it remains available as long as the user chooses to stay.

## Why Interactive SVG Is a Language Suited to Education
Interactive SVG is often considered merely a flexible or lightweight image format. In reality, once interactivity is introduced, SVG ceases to be an image in the traditional sense and becomes an environment. It does not scroll, does not advance automatically, does not lead the user from one point to another: it remains.
This permanence is one of its most relevant features in educational contexts. Unlike video, which imposes its own rhythm, interactive SVG grants the user complete control over duration. One can stop, go back, repeat an action, or observe a detail for as long as desired. Time is not a constraint but a resource, and learning can take place without the pressure of “keeping up.”
The interaction required is usually minimal: a click, a hover, a simple choice. Precisely this simplicity makes the action meaningful. The user is not asked to perform complex operations or demonstrate prior knowledge, but to carry out elementary gestures that produce visible consequences. Attention is not absorbed by the tool, but remains focused on content and on the relationship between action and result.
Another relevant aspect is the relationship with error.
In interactive SVG, error is rarely penalized. There are no points to lose, levels to fail, or answers to get wrong. The user can try, fail, go back, change their mind. This sanction-free exploration encourages a freer form of learning, based on direct experience rather than immediate verification.
Text also assumes a different role. It is not eliminated, but it ceases to be mandatory. It can appear on request, as an optional clarification or deepening, without interrupting the flow of experience. Those who wish to read can do so; those who prefer visual exploration are not excluded. Text becomes a possibility, not a command.
Interactive SVG is not a universal solution. It requires time, care, and a certain slowness in execution. It is not suitable for all content or all contexts.
Precisely for this reason, when used consciously, it can offer a type of educational experience that is difficult to replicate with other languages: one that unfolds through observation, action, and time shared with the object.
In this sense, rather than teaching, interactive SVG creates the conditions for learning.

## How This Project Embodies These Principles Without Declaring Itself Educational
The project discussed here was not conceived with the explicit aim of being educational. It does not propose structured learning paths, test competencies, or measure outcomes. Yet in the way it is designed and realized, it enacts many of the principles described above.
The experience is built as a space to be explored, not as a sequence to be completed. The user enters a scene that remains available and inhabitable over time. There is no explicit beginning and no mandatory ending. One can stop, go back, repeat actions, or simply observe.
Interactions are simple and produce immediate, visible consequences. Each gesture modifies something in the space, making the relationship between action and result clear. There is no absolute “right” combination and no failure to correct. Even system reactions, when present, do not judge but comment, leaving the user free to continue experimenting.
Textual content is optional. It does not interrupt or rigidly guide the experience. It can be ignored or consulted as a deepening layer. Different users can move through the same environment according to their own rhythms and modes.
Finally, the attention given to visual quality and detail is not decorative but relational. The care invested in the image is part of the pact with the user: if they are asked to offer time, attention, and curiosity, the space that welcomes them must be worthy of that request.
In this sense, the project does not teach something specific, but enacts a mode of experience that is compatible with education. It does not aim to replace other tools, but demonstrates how interactive SVG illustration can become a place where learning is possible precisely because it is not imposed.

## The Role of Drawing and Detail
In this project, drawing does not serve an illustrative function in the traditional sense. It does not explain content nor merely embellish an interface. Drawing is the place where the experience itself happens.
The choice of a slow, detailed, labor-intensive approach is not driven by realism or technical virtuosity. It is a deliberate stance. Each visual element is built to withstand prolonged observation and repeated presence, not just an immediate glance. The space must be able to be looked at multiple times without exhausting itself.
The level of detail has an ethical function before an aesthetic one. If the user is asked to give time, attention, and curiosity, the project must return something of equal weight. It cannot be poor, rushed, or merely functional. Visual richness becomes a form of respect.
Drawing is inseparable from interaction. Gestures have meaning because they take place within a coherent and carefully constructed visual world. Exploration is not driven by rewards or objectives, but by the desire to see what is there, what happens, what changes.
Detail is never neutral. Every object, stylistic reference, and graphic choice contributes to building an atmosphere that orients the experience without imposing it. Through this visual density, the project asks the user to slow down, observe, and remain.
If the experience works, it is also because drawing does not seek — or allow — shortcuts.

## Conclusion
This project does not originate as a response to a market demand, nor as a solution to a specific educational problem. It arises from a personal need to construct a space in which drawing, time, and interaction can coexist according to logics of quality rather than efficiency.
If it is presented today in relation to education, it is not to claim a role, but to recognize a compatibility. The experience does not teach directly, does not evaluate, does not measure. It asks only to enter, observe, act, and remain. It does not transmit content, but creates the conditions for something to happen.
The use of interactive SVG illustration demonstrates how it is possible to build visual environments that do not consume attention but hold it, that do not accelerate but slow down, that do not explain everything but leave room for experience. This approach is not suitable for every context, nor does it aim to replace more direct or structured tools. It is one possibility among others.
Above all, the project remains a gesture: an attempt to work with limited means without renouncing complexity, care, effort, and quality. A way of affirming that even outside the most established paths it is possible to construct meaningful experiences, provided one accepts the risk of not being immediately legible or easily classified.
If this work has value for others, it does not lie in its replicability, but in the question it leaves open:
what kind of space are we willing to offer when we ask someone for their time and attention?
